Ask a Year 10 class how to work out the file size of a bitmap image and
most of them will chant it back at you: width times height times colour
depth. Brilliant. Now give them a question that says "an image uses 16
colours" instead of "an image has a colour depth of 4 bits" and watch
half the room freeze.

That&apos;s the problem with bitmap images at GCSE. The formula is easy to
memorise and very easy to misapply, because students learn it as a
recipe rather than as a description of something real. They know the
words. They don&apos;t know what the words are pointing at.

This post is the sequence I use to fix that. It starts with something
students can see, builds up to the formula, and only names the formula
once they&apos;ve more or less worked it out for themselves.

## Where it goes wrong

Before planning the lesson, it&apos;s worth being honest about the mistakes
that actually cost marks. In my experience they come down to three.

- **Bits and bytes.** Students multiply width by height by colour depth,
  get a number in bits, and write it down as bytes. Or they divide by 8
  twice. Or they divide by 8 when the question asked for bits. The
  calculation is right and the answer is wrong.
- **Resolution versus size.** Students think resolution means how big the
  image looks on screen, so they assume a "larger" picture must have a
  higher resolution. Resolution is a count of pixels. How big those
  pixels are drawn is a separate question.
- **Colour depth versus number of colours.** This is the big one.
  Students hear "colour depth" and think it means how many colours there
  are. So when a question says "256 colours", they put 256 into the
  formula instead of 8. The answer comes out thirty-two times too big,
  and they don&apos;t notice, because they have no feel for what a sensible
  answer looks like.

All three have the same root. Students are treating the formula as
symbols to shuffle around rather than as a description of how an image
is stored. If they could picture the image being stored, none of these
mistakes would survive.

## Start with pixels you can see

So the first job is to make the pixels visible.

I open with a photo on the board and zoom in. And in. And in, until the
smooth picture falls apart into a grid of coloured squares. There is
always an audible reaction to this. Most students have never actually
looked at a pixel, even though they&apos;ve spent their whole lives staring
at them.

Then I hand out squared paper. Every student draws a small black and
white image, say 8 squares by 8 squares, by shading some squares and
leaving others blank. A smiley face, an arrow, their initial. Then the
key question:

> How would you describe your picture to someone over the phone, using
> only the words "zero" and "one"?

They get there quickly. Shaded is 1, blank is 0, read along each row.
Sixty-four squares, sixty-four digits. Each pixel needs exactly one bit
because it only has two possible states.

That&apos;s the whole foundation. A bitmap is a grid, each square stores a
number, and the number of bits depends on how many options each square
has. Everything else in the topic is a variation on that idea.

Before moving on, I ask one more question: if I sent you my sixty-four
digits, how would you know whether to draw them as 8 by 8 or 4 by 16?
You can&apos;t. You need the width and height as well. That&apos;s **metadata**,
and introducing it here, as a problem they&apos;ve just bumped into, makes
it far more memorable than a definition on a slide.

## Colour depth is a choice

Next, I take the black and white image and ask: what if I want four
colours instead of two?

One bit gives us two options, 0 and 1. Two bits give us four: 00, 01,
10 and 11. So each pixel now needs two bits. Three bits give eight
options, and so on. I build the table on the board with the class
calling out the next row:

| Bits per pixel | Colours |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | 2 |
| 2 | 4 |
| 3 | 8 |
| 4 | 16 |
| 8 | 256 |
| 24 | 16,777,216 |

By the third row, someone usually spots the pattern: each extra bit
doubles the number of colours. That&apos;s 2ⁿ, and they found it themselves.

The framing that matters here is that **colour depth is a design
decision**. Someone chose how many bits each pixel gets. More bits means
more colours and a better-looking image, but every pixel gets more
expensive to store. Once students see it as a trade-off rather than a
fact to learn, the reverse question ("how many bits do you need for 16
colours?") stops being scary. They just run the table backwards.

It also helps to show what happens when you get the trade-off wrong.
Drop a photo from 24-bit down to 4-bit and smooth gradients collapse
into bands of flat colour. That&apos;s posterisation, and seeing it once is
worth more than any written explanation.

## Building the formula together

Now, and only now, we work out file size.

Back to the squared-paper image. It&apos;s 8 by 8 pixels, and each pixel
needs 1 bit. How many bits altogether? Students work it out without
needing a formula: 64 pixels, 1 bit each, 64 bits.

Now make it a 4-colour image. Same grid, but each pixel needs 2 bits. 128
bits. Now make it 16 by 16 with 4 colours. 256 pixels, 2 bits each, 512
bits.

At this point I ask the class to write down a general rule in their own
words. What they write is nearly always some version of "count the
pixels, then multiply by how many bits each one needs." Then I put the
formal version next to it:

**file size in bits = width × height × colour depth**

It&apos;s the same thing. They already understood it before they saw it.

Then one worked example, done slowly:

> An image is 100 × 200 pixels with a colour depth of 8 bits.
>
> 100 × 200 = 20,000 pixels.
> 20,000 × 8 = 160,000 bits.
> 160,000 ÷ 8 = 20,000 bytes.

After that, I go straight to twist questions, because that&apos;s where real
understanding shows:

- What happens to the file size if you double the width and the height?
  (It goes up four times, not two. This one catches almost everyone.)
- What happens if you halve the colour depth?
- An image uses 16 colours. What is its colour depth?
- Two images have the same file size. One is 200 × 200 with 8-bit colour.
  The other is 400 × 400. What is its colour depth?

None of these need a new formula. They just need students to think
about pixels and bits per pixel, which is exactly the point.

## Units and where the marks go

The final step is the one that quietly costs the most marks: converting
the answer into the units the question asks for.

The rules are short, so I give them as a checklist and make students
use it every time:

1. The formula gives you **bits**.
2. Divide by 8 to get **bytes**.
3. Divide by 1,000 (or 1,024) to get **kilobytes**, and again for
   **megabytes**.
4. Before writing the answer, check: does the unit you&apos;ve written match
   the unit the question asked for?

On step 3, check what your exam board expects. Specifications have
moved towards using 1,000 for kilo, with 1,024 reserved for kibibytes
(KiB), and some boards accept either in calculations. Whatever your board
says, teach one method consistently and make students write the unit at
every line of working. Most unit errors disappear once the unit is
written down next to each number.

It&apos;s also worth doing a quick sense check together. A small icon should
be a few kilobytes. A phone photo, uncompressed, should be several
megabytes. If a student&apos;s answer says a 100 × 100 icon is 2 gigabytes,
they should feel that something has gone wrong before they move on.

Finally, remind students that the formula ignores metadata and
compression. Real files are a bit bigger because of the header, and
usually much smaller because of compression. Exam questions will say if
they want you to account for either.

## Ready-to-use resources

I built an interactive version of this whole sequence as a free lesson:
[Bitmap Images](/bitmap-images). It runs in the browser with nothing to
install, and it follows the same order as this post:

- **Zoom in on pixels.** A slider zooms into a photo until the grid
  appears.
- **Build your own pixel art.** Students paint a grid with a fixed
  palette, which is the squared-paper activity in digital form.
- **Drop the resolution.** A slider reduces a photo&apos;s resolution so
  students can watch it go blocky.
- **Drop the colour depth.** The same photo, posterising as the bit depth
  falls.
- **File size calculator.** Enter a width, height and colour depth and see
  the answer in bits, bytes and kilobytes.
- **Five-question quiz.** A quick check at the end, including a colour
  depth question that targets the "colours versus bits" mistake.

It works well on the board as a demo, or with students working through
it on their own machines while you circulate.

## The bigger point

Bitmap images are a small topic, but they show something that applies
across most of GCSE Computer Science. Students don&apos;t struggle because
the maths is hard. Multiplying three numbers and dividing by 8 is not
hard. They struggle because the thing being described is invisible, so
the formula feels arbitrary.

Make the invisible thing visible first, let them build the rule from
what they can see, and the formula becomes a summary of something they
already understand rather than a spell to recite. That&apos;s worth doing
for almost every topic in data representation.
