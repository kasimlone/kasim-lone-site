Walk into any English classroom mid-lesson. The teacher can glance around,
open OneNote, flick through a dozen student pages in under a minute and
know exactly who is stuck, who is racing ahead, and who has written the
same sentence three times because they don&apos;t know what to do next.

Now walk into a Computer Science classroom during a coding task. Thirty
screens. Thirty IDEs. Thirty half-written programs, most of them not
saved anywhere the teacher can actually reach. You can walk laps of the
room, you can lean over shoulders, and by the end of the hour you still
won&apos;t have seen more than about a third of the class&apos;s work.

That gap is the single biggest reason coding lessons are harder to run
well than most other lessons in the school, and it&apos;s the reason I built
[RunPy](https://www.runpy.co.uk).

## Written work is visible by default

Written work has a very particular property that we take for granted:
it&apos;s **laid flat**. A book is one page at a time. A OneNote section is a
list of student names down the side. You click, you scan, you click the
next name. The overhead of checking one more student is close to zero.

That property is why formative assessment works on paper. You can walk
the room, scan a page in three seconds, ask the right question, and
move on. Multiply that by thirty and you&apos;ve done more meaningful
feedback in a lesson than most e-learning platforms manage in a term.

Code doesn&apos;t behave like that. Code sits inside an IDE, on a machine,
in a file, sometimes on a network drive that mysteriously logs the
student out. There is no "flick through the class" gesture. The default
state of code, in a normal school setup, is **hidden**.

## What you actually lose

When you can&apos;t see the class&apos;s code, you lose things you probably
don&apos;t notice you&apos;ve lost:

- **The quiet stuck kid.** The one who won&apos;t raise a hand. In written
  work you spot a mostly-blank page from three tables away. In an IDE
  they can sit there with an empty file and a blinking cursor and you
  never see it unless you happen to walk past.
- **The misconception spreading in real time.** When four students all
  make the same off-by-one error in the first five minutes, that&apos;s a
  reteach opportunity. If you don&apos;t see it until you mark work later,
  it&apos;s already baked in.
- **Live feedback that means anything.** "Let me have a look" is a
  brilliant sentence when you can actually see the code. It&apos;s a much
  weaker sentence when you have to lean over a shoulder, ask them to
  scroll, and squint at a 12pt font on a screen at a bad angle.
- **An honest picture at the end of the lesson.** Ask any CS teacher how
  many of the class actually finished the task. Most of us are guessing.
  We&apos;ve seen a sample and extrapolated.
- **Marking that reflects the process, not just the final file.** Code
  that&apos;s handed in tells you what worked. Code you watched being
  written tells you what they understood.

None of these are exotic problems. They&apos;re the ordinary business of
teaching. We just don&apos;t get to do them properly with code because the
tools weren&apos;t built for a classroom.

## Why generic IDEs make it worse

A professional IDE is designed for one developer, alone, with their own
files, their own settings, and their own sense of what a good workflow
looks like. Every one of those defaults works against a teacher:

- Files live on the local machine, or on a mapped drive that only that
  student is logged into.
- There&apos;s no concept of "the class". Just the current user.
- Nothing is shared until the student explicitly shares it, usually as
  an attachment in an email or a folder submission at the end of the
  lesson.
- The teacher gets nothing until the student volunteers it.

Some schools patch around this with screen-monitoring software, which
lets you look at any one student&apos;s screen at a time. That&apos;s useful
for behaviour. It is not the same as seeing the class&apos;s code. Watching
a video feed of a text editor is not scanning a page.

## What a fix has to do

For a coding lesson to feel like a normal, well-run lesson, you need
four things:

1. Every student&apos;s current code, live, on one screen for the teacher.
2. The ability to open a student&apos;s work and **run it** exactly as they
   wrote it, without emails or file transfers.
3. Inline feedback that lands back in the student&apos;s workspace, not in
   a separate document they&apos;ll never open.
4. Low enough friction that a student can just start coding. If setup
   takes ten minutes, you&apos;ve already lost the lesson.

That is the specification. Everything else is detail.

## The bigger point

The reason CS lessons often feel harder to run than other lessons
isn&apos;t that the content is harder (though it is), or that the kids are
less engaged (they usually aren&apos;t). It&apos;s that the tools we&apos;ve
inherited were built for a different job. Professional IDEs are
brilliant for professional work. In a classroom, they hide exactly the
thing a teacher most needs to see.

Give teachers the same visibility over code that they already have over
written work, and the rest of the lesson looks a lot more like the rest
of the timetable. That&apos;s the whole idea.
